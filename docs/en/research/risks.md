# Risk assessment and mitigation

<a id="3-risk-assessment"></a>

## 3. Risk assessment {#risks}

This register lists eighteen risks for learners who practise programming with an agentic coding assistant such as Claude Code. It covers two groups: students who prepare for an exam without AI, and junior developers at work. §4 sets out how to reduce each risk.

Likelihood (L) and Impact (I) are scored 1–5, and Score = L × I. All scores were judged for one scenario: a learner who practises with an agentic coding assistant and uses **no mitigation**. R15 is the exception: its likelihood assumes the harness, as its evidence cell says.

- **Judgements, not measurements:** the scores are judgements. The evidence grade in brackets (A–D, [§2.0](literature.md#20-scope-method-and-evidence-grades)) shows how well each mechanism is supported. A high score with weak evidence is a reason to watch closely, not a fact.
- **Horizon:** *Exam* is the assessment without AI at the end of a course. *Career* is work after studies, including junior developers at work. *Both* means the risk applies on both horizons. R8 and R15–R18 relate to [the shift in skill needs](skill-shift.md) (§2.9): which skills still pay off as AI writes more code.
- **Levels:** L0–L4 in the text are the levels of the harness's [Autonomy Ladder](design.md#52-the-autonomy-ladder-per-skill), from L0 (demonstration) to L4 (automated). They are not the L column, which is likelihood.

**Reading the register.**

- **Top risks:** R1 (skills never form) and R14 (practice–test mismatch) are joint highest. R14 is not an AI risk as such, but in the pilot it is the one that actually happened (see the example below).
- **Asymmetric skill-shift risks:** too much hand-writing (R15) is a small risk for a learner who uses the harness, because the harness already hands mastered skills to the AI, and an exam without AI requires hand-writing. Too little supervision practice (R16) is a real risk. Nothing in the harness trains it yet; §4.1 shows how a learner could add it.

!!! example "Example: one student's situation"
    A second-year HBO-ICT student at CHE practised for an OOD exam with AI at hand, untimed, in Visual Studio. A diagnostic test before the exam was timed, without AI and in Rider. Its analysis named the practice conditions as a main cause of the result, and debugging was where it went wrong. Time had also gone to a cheat sheet instead of practice (R12).

## 4. How to apply it: mitigation plan {#mitigation}

This section turns the risks into a plan. The table gives one mitigation for each risk, what implements it, and the residual risk: the level expected with the mitigation in place, again a judgement. Many mitigations are features of the harness, built on its [hook pipeline](design.md#54-hook-pipeline); the others are habits of practice. The step plan after the table is written for a student and counts from the exam.

| Risk | Mitigation | Implemented by | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | [Autonomy Ladder](design.md#52-the-autonomy-ladder-per-skill): the AI may write code for a skill only at L0 (demonstration) and L4 (automated). At L1–L3 a `PreToolUse` hook blocks AI edits to code the learner owns. | Skill record + `PreToolUse` hook + CLAUDE.md policy | Low |
| R2 | The mastery gate needs (a) independent success on a **novel transfer task**, (b) a correct **explanation** in the learner's own words, (c) success spread over ≥ 2 separate days. The diary shows hints used vs. independent work. | `/mastery-check` skill; diary statistics | Low–medium |
| R3 | `SessionStart` asks for a plan ("what will you try today?"). The `Stop` hook triggers a reflection that *the learner* writes. Hints are Socratic questions first. | Hooks + diary | Medium |
| R4 | OOD skills start at **"learner proposes, AI critiques"**. The learner draws the class diagram or names the responsibilities *before* the AI comments. Trade-off questions are required ("why composition here?"). | `/learn` skill protocol for the design category | Medium |
| R5 | [Hint ladder](design.md#53-friction-calibration-the-hint-ladder) with time limits and an attempt requirement. **Escape hatch** `/override <reason>` is allowed, but it gets logged and the skill is flagged. Overrides are reviewed weekly in the diary. The learner treats the system as a self-chosen commitment device. Students bypass guardrails most under deadline pressure ([Kapoor et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11146)), so practice is planned so that the crunch before a test is not when hints are needed most. | `UserPromptSubmit` hook; diary | Medium (cannot be eliminated) |
| R6 | Stuck timer: after about 10–15 minutes without progress, the next hint rung unlocks automatically. Consolidation (worked solution plus explanation) comes after a failed attempt. Target success rate is about 85%; the skill gets *easier* tasks if the learner falls below 70%. | `UserPromptSubmit` hook + session state | Low |
| R7 | Help level is set *per skill*, not globally. Promotion criteria move the learner up quickly when evidence is strong. | Skill record | Low |
| R8 | [Spaced maintenance checks](design.md#57-spaced-reinforcement-after-mastery) on expanding intervals (e.g. 3 → 7 → 21 → 60 → 120 days). A failed check demotes the skill to L3. At L4 the learner must predict or review the AI's approach on samples. | `SessionStart` hook + `/review-due` skill | Low |
| R9 | The AI must cite docs, and the learner runs the tests (`check.mjs`). AI code and explanations are treated as untrusted. Interfaces, abstract classes and inheritance, where AI is weakest, need extra care, as does anything security-related: the learner cross-checks those with the course material or the C# docs. Explanation errors the learner finds get logged as "AI errors". | CLAUDE.md, `check.mjs` | Medium |
| R10 | The diary records what *the learner* wrote. Before handing in, the learner rehearses defending the design with `/mastery-check --defend`. | Diary + skill | Low |
| R11 | Graded work ([§10.8](build-log.md#108-revision-of-13-november-2026-graded-work-under-the-exam-rules)): the learner names its paths with `setting graded`. There the hint ladder goes up to rung 4 for every skill, a worked solution is never available, and the guard blocks every write by Claude, also outside the project. The hooks keep an AI-use log in the repo: a row per answer, an export of each session (Claude Code has no share link), and an APA reference. The learner fills in what they did with the help and how they checked it (as CHE's OER art. 7.11 asks, for example). The learner asks the teacher which parts of the final assessment are level 0 and which are level 1 (§4.2). Course and institution rules always apply. | Hooks (guard, prompt-gate, Stop) + `ai-verantwoording/` | Low |
| R12 | The harness stays small (~5 hooks, 1 skill) and is reviewed monthly. Harness work is time-boxed: at most one fix per session, and research only when it would change what the learner practises. Research about the harness counts as harness work too: it waits until after the exam, unless a hook misfires. | Diary (minutes spent on the harness vs. practice) | Medium |
| R13 | `.learning/`, `research_notes/` and transcripts stay out of shared repos (`.gitignore`), and only what is needed is stored. Publishing is the learner's choice, and then only a filtered selection goes online: the dashboard export takes listed fields only and filters the learner's private terms (`.learning/private.json`), a security check runs before every push, and the learner reads the diff first. The diary says that reflections appear on the public dashboard. The pilot student chose to publish such a selection. | `.gitignore`, `export.mjs`, `security-check.mjs` | Low |
| R14 | **Practise under test conditions.** From about two weeks before each test, the learner does timed sessions without AI in the IDE of the exam: short ones first, then full mock exams (the course's practice tests). Early sessions are low-stakes. Afterwards the learner reviews the session and uses `claude-study` only for what they found themselves. | Step plan below; diary | Medium |
| R15 | No drills for skills already at L3/L4. The Fibonacci reviews keep them fresh, and boilerplate goes to the AI (L4). Hand practice goes to skills below L3. | Skill register; L4 policy | Low |
| R16 | **A supervision track (§4.1):** finding planted bugs in code the learner did not write, writing tests that catch mistakes, reviewing AI code against OOD principles, predicting and then comparing. Only on topics at L3 or higher, so the learner can judge the output. Before the exam, only the part that overlaps with the exam (debugging, testing). After the exam, the full track: acceptance criteria that become tests, domain models in UML, and judging AI-built work against them. | New microskills (added by the learner); exercise code from Claude in `voorbeelden/` or the chat | Medium |
| R17 | Judgement that employers can see: tests and a test plan, design rationale in the report, explaining one's code aloud (oral defence). | Final project; diary | Medium (outside the learner's control) |
| R18 | Decisions follow the evidence grades (§2.0): A/B evidence is acted on, C is treated as a signal and D as a claim. Important figures are checked in the primary source, keeping in mind who wrote this document. This review is re-run only when it could change what the learner practises. | This document | Low–medium |

### 4.1 Rebalancing practice: an example of register changes {#41-rebalancing-practice-proposed-register-changes-you-decide}

The harness already does two of the three things [§2.9](skill-shift.md) recommends: it keeps hand practice for skills below mastery, and it hands mastered skills to the AI (L4). What is missing is supervision (R16). A learner can add it to their own skill register. The box shows the changes proposed to the student from the example in §3. They are proposals: the learner decides whether to make them.

!!! example "Example: one student's register changes"
    | When | What | Why |
    |---|---|---|
    | Until the exam | The student keeps the exam plan: closing the gaps in the core OOD skills by hand, and from about two weeks before the exam, timed mocks without AI in the IDE of the exam (R14). The student adds only the overlap between supervision and the exam: **debugging code someone else wrote**, and **writing tests that catch mistakes**. | The student first checks whether the exam grades them. In this student's case, the assessment graded a test plan, and debugging was where the diagnostic test went wrong. AI-era interviews test both too. |
    | After the exam | A new top-level skill for supervising AI output. It covers:<br>• specifying a task or user story precisely enough for an agent or a team;<br>• writing acceptance criteria that become tests;<br>• reviewing AI-built work against them and against OOD principles;<br>• predicting what the AI will produce, then comparing. | The career horizon (§2.9). This is not material for an exam without AI, so it does not belong before the exam. |

    **Example commands.** The student runs them, because the guard does not let Claude change the register:

    ```
    ! node .claude/hooks/skill.mjs add-micro debug.zonder-foutmelding debug.code-van-ander L1 "bug vinden in code die je niet zelf schreef" "geplante bug, code van een ander, ai-code debuggen"
    ! node .claude/hooks/skill.mjs add-micro test.unit-tests test.fouten-vangen L1 "tests schrijven die fouten in andermans code vangen" "test die fout vangt, ai-code testen, grenswaarde vergeten"
    ```

    Claude writes the exercise code, with a planted bug, in `voorbeelden/` or in the chat. The student debugs it in their own exercise folder, so `check.mjs` can log the result. Only topics already at L3 or higher are used, so the student can judge the code. A top-level skill such as `ai.supervisie` cannot be added with `add-micro`. The student would add it to `skills.json` by hand after the exam.

### 4.2 For teachers and programmes

This part is for teachers, curriculum designers and exam committees. Each point gives the evidence and ends with a recommendation. The research for this review found that supporting AI-free assessment of core skills, and closing the gaps around it, is a stronger position than arguing against it.

1. **Assess core skills at AI level 0, under supervision.** This is the consensus, not an outlier.
    - **Matches the two-lane model:** it is "lane 1" in the Dutch national vision ([Npuls, 2025](https://npuls.nl/kennisbank/visie-op-toetsing-examinering-en-ai-handreikingen)) and in the University of Sydney's two-lane model. Sydney's Green Paper also requires foundational skills before AI-supported tasks and "opportunities for performance independent of AI" (University of Sydney, 2026).
    - **Why supervision is needed:** a ban on AI in unsupervised work cannot be verified. In one blind test, 94% of AI-written exam answers went undetected ([Scarfe et al., 2024](references.md#ref-scarfe-2024)).
    - **Why AI-assisted work overstates mastery:** AI-assisted assignments averaged 7.62, while mastery checked at random without devices averaged 5.55 ([Đức Minh, 2026](https://doi.org/10.32674/nrmwyw65); grade C).
    - *Recommendation:* state for each part of the final assessment whether it is level 0 or level 1, and tell students before they start practising.
2. **AI-allowed work is only as strong as how it is checked.** Rules about permitted use are "discursive" changes that students can ignore ([Corbin, Dawson & Liu, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2025.2503964)). What makes them work is structure: prompt links and records of AI use (CHE's OER art. 7.11 requires them, for example), and an oral check.
    - **The oral defence:** it targets a risk from §3: students who cannot explain or defend their own work (R10). Tracing and explaining code predict writing ability ([Lopez et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1145/1404520.1404531)), so explaining is a fair test of skill. Requiring an explanation also helps during practice: on a later task with AI blacked out, 77% failed after unrestricted AI, against 39% after an "explanation gate" ([Sankaranarayanan, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20206); grade C).
    - **A gap in the evidence:** none of the studies in this review tests the oral defence itself as an assessment format.
    - *Recommendation:* say in every level-1 assignment how it is verified, for example prompt links, an AI-use log and a short oral defence.
3. **Give lane 2 a place in the programme, and grade supervision of AI output.** Npuls says the two lanes "complement each other", and employers now test reading, debugging and validating AI output (Google's 2026 interview pilot). Students have to *acquire* supervision skills, not just keep them ("never-skilling"; [Abdulnour, Gin & Boscardin, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMra2503232)). A rule such as CHE's art. 7.11 already makes students responsible for checking AI output "op juistheid en relevantie" (for correctness and relevance). That could be assessed as a skill, not only as compliance.
    - **No cost to exam results:** CS1-LLM shows that AI-integrated practice can coexist with AI-free exams without worse exam results.
    - **Exercise formats with early evidence (grade C):** realistic bugs injected into AI-generated solutions made students repair the code itself, while natural prompt failures made them reword the prompt ([Pădurean et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05068)). "Prompt Problems" train precise specification ([Denny et al., 2024b](https://doi.org/10.1145/3626252.3630909)).
    - **Order matters:** foundations first, then supervision practice on topics students can already write themselves, so they can judge the output ([P13](design.md#211-synthesis-design-principles)).
    - *Recommendation:* name at least one course or assignment at level 2 where checking AI output is graded, and place it after the core skills are in place.
4. **Let students practise under exam conditions.** Practice transfers best when it matches the test ([§2.6](literature.md#26-practice-conditions-and-transfer-to-the-test)). Keep it low-stakes, though: high-stakes quizzes during learning removed the benefit of retrieval practice ([Hinze & Rapp, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.3032)). No study has measured the cost of an unfamiliar IDE, but creating classes, running tests and reading compiler output are part of the procedure being tested. The example in §3 shows the gap: practice with AI in one IDE, then a diagnostic test without AI, timed, in another.
    - *Recommendation:* make practice tests available in the exam environment beforehand (same IDE, same time limit, no AI), and keep them low-stakes.
5. **Do not overclaim.**
    - **AI Act:** EU AI Act Article 4 (AI literacy) is about the staff of providers and deployers. Since 27 July 2026 it only requires that organisations *support* AI literacy. It does not require AI in student assessment.
    - **Labour market:** the evidence on junior jobs is contested.
    - **Code-share figures:** vendor figures about how much code AI writes are claims, not evidence.
    - **Learning gains:** after correcting for publication bias, the most rigorous 2026 meta-analysis shrinks g = 0.84 to 0.08 ([Boolzen et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10462-026-11665-9)).
    - *Recommendation:* base programme changes on grade A/B evidence (§2.0), and treat vendor figures as claims.
6. **Detection is not proof.** Npuls states that AI-detection scores are not watertight proof and that suspicions need human investigation, for example oral questioning. CHE is one institution that allows Turnitin in fraud investigations. Students' own prompt links and records protect them.
    - *Recommendation:* treat a detection score as a reason for a conversation, not as evidence, and ask students to keep their prompt links and records.
7. **See a tool like this harness as an example** of transparent, self-limited AI use. It logs hints, overrides and explanations, which is the kind of AI-use statement level-1 work asks for. If a course offers its own AI tutor, the evidence favours guardrails built into the workflow over instructions in a prompt.
    - **Prompts alone:** steering a tutor through its system prompt made no difference for 1,059 CS1 students ([Barth et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3765964.3811656)). A guard-railed tutor helped only inside a mastery workflow ([Oreopoulos et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.26300/01qv-6c22)).
    - **Equity:** weaker and first-generation students are hurt more and use escape hatches more ([Liu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.26300/y3f8-vh05); [Kapoor et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11146)). Guardrails matter most for the students least likely to choose them.
    - *Recommendation:* consider accepting logs like these as part of an AI-use statement. When choosing an AI tutor, ask where its guardrails are enforced.
