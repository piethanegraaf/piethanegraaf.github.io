# Generative AI and the labour market for software developers and programmers (US, EU, Netherlands), as of 7 November 2026

> **Labels used on every finding.** Timing: **[NEW yyyy-mm-dd]** = first published between 1 May and 7 Nov 2026 (date checked on the source unless flagged); **[BG yyyy-mm]** = dated background from 2025 to April 2026. Design: **CAUSAL-DESIGN** (difference-in-differences, event study or a similar identification strategy; still observational, not an experiment), **CORRELATIONAL**, **DESCRIPTIVE**, **FORECAST**. Source type: **WP** = working paper (not peer-reviewed), **GOV** = government statistic, agency or central-bank publication, **IND** = industry report, **PRACT** = Dutch practitioner journal ESB (edited, not academic peer review), **PRESS**. **COI** = conflict of interest. "(unverified)" = only seen in search snippets or secondary coverage; I could not open the primary source. None of the core studies below was verified as peer-reviewed by 7 Nov 2026.

## 1. Main studies on early-career software-developer employment since late 2022, and what is new in May–Nov 2026

### Takeaway
In US payroll and administrative data, employment of 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations, software development among them, has diverged sharply from less-exposed peers since late 2022. The Stanford "Canaries" gap widened from 15% (July 2025 data) to 19% (June 2026 data), and it works mainly through less hiring of young workers, not layoffs. Economy-wide trackers (Yale Budget Lab, through August 2026) and the Danish administrative-data study still show no aggregate effect. US software-development postings recovered in 2026 (+23.8% year on year by 30 Oct 2026), but that recovery is senior- and AI-heavy: only 4.5% of US software-development postings were entry-level in Q1 2026.

### Cited Findings

**Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" (Stanford Digital Economy Lab; ADP payroll data)**

- [NEW 2026-08-12] WP, DESCRIPTIVE (the authors' own label). Revised paper using ADP payroll data "covering millions of U.S. workers through June 2026". Employment of workers aged 22–25 in AI-exposed occupations "now stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with that of their less-exposed peers"; "experienced workers show no comparable gap"; no evidence of "widespread, economy-wide job displacement" — [Stanford DEL, Canaries Aug 2026 PDF](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf); [Stanford DEL news, 12 Aug 2026](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/)
- [NEW 2026-08-12] Trend: by the same "kept pace" measure, the shortfall was 15% at the July 2025 data vintage and 19% at June 2026, i.e. the gap "has widened steadily since we first documented it in August 2025" — [Canaries Aug 2026 PDF](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf)
- [NEW 2026-08-12] Levels: from November 2022 to June 2026, employment of 22–25-year-olds in the two most exposed quintiles fell about 11%, while in the three least exposed quintiles it grew about 10% — [Stanford DEL news, footnote 1](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/)
- [NEW 2026-08-12] Regression form: for ages 22–25, employment in the most exposed quintile fell about 18 percentage points relative to the least exposed quintile between Nov 2022 and June 2026 (coefficient −0.179). - [NEW 2026-08-12] Mechanism: the adjustment runs "primarily through reduced hiring of young workers rather than increased separations". Declines concentrate where AI usage "primarily substitutes for human tasks"; where it complements workers, employment is "flat or rising, especially for experienced workers". Adjustment shows up in employment, not base pay — [Canaries Aug 2026 PDF, abstract](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf)

### Inferences
- Three independent US administrative or payroll sources point the same way for young workers in exposed occupations: ADP (Canaries, −19% vs the counterfactual), Census QWI (Tucker, −12%; see Q2) and Revelio résumés (junior +6% vs senior +31% at adopters). The magnitudes differ a lot by data source; see Q2 for the ACS benchmark, where the gap is close to zero.
- For an entrant in 2028–2029 the most decision-relevant US fact is compositional. Even in a posting recovery, entry-level software roles were 4.5% of postings and 71% of the 2025–2026 increase was senior. A rising tide in developer demand has not lifted junior hiring proportionally so far.
- European posting indices (Germany and France at new lows in Jul–Oct 2026) are weaker than the US. The US 2026 rebound should not be assumed to transfer to the Dutch market; see Q4 for Dutch data.

### Gaps
- No Netherlands-specific Indeed software-development index was found, either on FRED or published by Indeed Hiring Lab.
- Canaries dashboard values after June 2026 were not checked. The software-developer-only ADP series (levels by age) is in the online appendix, which I did not extract.
- The NY Fed by-major table (Feb 2026, 2024 data) could not be opened directly, so the 7.8%/7.0% figures rest on consistent secondary reporting.
- The Hosseini Maasoum & Lichtinger May 2026 revision, with its reported ~9% figure, could not be verified.

## 2. How strong is the causal case that AI, rather than over-hiring, interest rates, Section 174, offshoring, remote work or the cycle, drives these trends?

### Takeaway
The case is **suggestive but not established**. Studies with the most careful designs aimed at coders find an occupation-specific slowdown starting around ChatGPT's release that industry shocks and interest-rate exposure do not explain: coder employment growth about 3 percentage points a year lower (Crane & Soto, Fed Board) and a 12% early-career employment drop in the most-exposed industries (Census). A strong set of null and alternative-explanation results stands against this: precise Danish nulls; no spike in recent-graduate unemployment in summer 2026; remote work explaining 64% of the rise in young-graduate unemployment; real interest rates explaining most variation in software-engineer hiring; declines that started before ChatGPT; and a much smaller gap in the national ACS than in ADP. Section 174 and offshoring have no direct quantitative tests in this literature; indirect tests mostly speak against them as the main driver.

### Cited Findings

**Evidence for an AI-specific (occupation- or age-specific) component**

- [BG 2026-03-20] GOV/WP (Federal Reserve Board FEDS 2026-018), CAUSAL-DESIGN (event study with an industry-shock counterfactual). Linking O*NET to the CPS, "aggregate employment of coders has decelerated sharply since the introduction of ChatGPT". After controlling for industry-level shocks, "coder employment growth has been 3 percent lower since the introduction of ChatGPT", which the authors read as an "occupation-specific shock". Coder employment "has continued to grow in recent years, though much more slowly than it did pre-2022" — [Crane & Soto, FEDS 2026-018](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026018pap.pdf); [abstract page](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/ai-and-coder-employment-compiling-the-evidence.htm)
- [BG 2026-03-20] Same paper: "much of the debate over AI and the labor market has centered on industry-level stories such as interest-rate sensitivity and post-Covid dynamics", but "the relevant industries differentially substituted away from coder employment". Results are qualitatively unchanged when software developers are excluded, when NAICS 5415 is excluded, and with smoothed CPS weights. The authors caveat that this "may reflect reallocation of tasks across occupations" — [Crane & Soto](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026018pap.pdf)
- [BG 2026-04; page revised 2026-05-07] GOV/WP (US Census Bureau CES-WP-26-27), CAUSAL-DESIGN (event study, triple differences, local projections). Using Quarterly Workforce Indicators, ChatGPT's introduction is followed by "an immediate, sizable, and persistent drop in hires" of 22–24-year-olds in the most AI-exposed industry-state cells. Regression-adjusted early-career employment in the most exposed quintile fell 12% over the 10 quarters after ChatGPT; hiring "largely recovered by early 2025", but on a smaller employment base. On monetary policy, high-exposure industries "are not especially sensitive" to monetary shocks. A historical decomposition attributes "up to one quarter" of the relative early-career decline through 2025Q2 to monetary policy shocks through 2023, and these shocks "do not explain the rapid hiring decline at the most AI-exposed firms". There is some evidence of earlier trend shifts around the onset of COVID-19 — [Tucker, Census CES-WP-26-27](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-27.html)
- [NEW 2026-08-12] WP, DESCRIPTIVE robustness (Canaries). Controlling for occupational interest-rate exposure barely changes the young-worker estimate (−0.178 vs −0.179). AI exposure and interest-rate exposure are negatively correlated overall. The result is "essentially unchanged when dropping computer occupations and only slightly smaller when dropping technology firms" (NAICS 51 and 5415). On **Section 174**: the 2022 change "may have restrained hiring in technology-intensive occupations", but "the robustness of our results to excluding technology firms and computer occupations also speaks against this channel". On **remote work and offshoring**: the gap also appears in non-teleworkable occupations, "suggest[ing] that our findings are not driven by outsourcing or work-from-home disruptions, at least solely". The decline is sharpest in "leading-adoption states" (about −19% for the most exposed quintile), using state usage data from the Anthropic Economic Index (COI: vendor data) — [Canaries Aug 2026 PDF](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf)
- [BG 2026-03] GOV (Dallas Fed, Atkinson & Yamco). There is "some correlation across occupations between employment declines and AI exposure, but only for younger workers", driven by fewer people moving from outside the labour force into jobs, not by layoffs — [SF Fed/Dallas Fed System Research, Mar 2026](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/system-research-dallas-fed/2026/03/young-workers-employment-drops-occupations-high-ai-exposure/) (from search snippet)
- [BG 2026-02-17] GOV (Fed Governor Barr, speech). Cites ADP research that early-career workers in exposed jobs such as software developers and customer service representatives saw relative employment declines, while concluding that AI has not yet had a substantial effect on aggregate employment — [Federal Reserve Board](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/barr20260217a.htm) (from search snippet)

**Weaknesses inside the AI-supportive studies**

- [NEW 2026-08-12] Canaries: adding occupational college share roughly halves the most-exposed-quintile estimate (−0.18 to −0.09, the latter significant only at the 10% level). There are "differential trends between more and less exposed occupations before ChatGPT, particularly during the COVID-19 Pandemic". In the **national ACS** the 22–25 gap between most and least exposed quintiles is only −0.022, with a CI that includes zero (−0.019 for full-time civilian wage and salary workers; −0.047 using unweighted respondent counts). The authors conclude that "the magnitude of the divergence appears specific to the ADP sample, but its direction is consistent", and warn against "extrapolating them to the economy as a whole" — [Canaries Aug 2026 PDF](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf)
- [NEW 2026-07-28] Revelio: adopting firms "were already growing faster" before adoption, so adopter-based comparisons such as Hosseini Maasoum & Lichtinger's are not clean experiments — [Revelio Labs](https://www.reveliolabs.com/news/ai-and-work/introduction-the-revelio-ai-labor-market-tracker)

**Evidence for non-AI drivers or null effects**

- [NEW 2026-06-01] GOV (NY Fed Liberty Street Economics), CORRELATIONAL decomposition. "We estimate that remote work can explain 64 percent of the recent increase in unemployment among young college graduates"; "the timing of this surge suggests that remote work—not generative AI—explains the bulk of the rise". The proposed mechanism is harder training and mentoring of new hires in distributed teams — [Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, 1 Jun 2026](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/06/remote-work-leaves-younger-workers-sidelined/). PRESS: in "remotable" jobs (example: software development), unemployment of college graduates aged 28 or younger rose about 1 percentage point between 2017–2019 and 2022–2024, while it fell slightly for those 29 and over — [Fortune/AP, 1 Jun 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/06/01/new-fed-study-remote-work-ai-driving-higher-unemployment/)
- [NEW 2026-09] WP (NBER w35796), CAUSAL-DESIGN (difference-in-differences and event study vs older graduates and young non-graduates, CPS June–August 2026). Recent-graduate unemployment did not spike in summer 2026 relative to prior summers or to either comparison group. Counting "sidelined" workers who want a job raises the rate by nearly 2 percentage points, with no significant relative increase. There is "some evidence" of a positive relationship between 2026 unemployment and remote-work availability — [Fairlie & Wu, NBER w35796](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35796). (unverified secondary numbers: summer-2026 rate 7.3% vs a 6.3–7.8% range over the previous four summers; 10.4% on the broader measure; sample too small to rule out increases of 2–3 points — [UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/for-new-college-grads-the-ai-jobpocalypse-didnt-arrive/))
- [NEW 2026-07-16 / 2026-09-15] Yale Budget Lab: no clear AI footprint in occupational churn, exposure of the unemployed, or a synthetic DiD through August 2026 — [Yale Budget Lab](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/page_to_pdf/1419/publication_1419.pdf)
- [BG 2026-03] Humlum & Vestergaard (Denmark): effects on earnings and hours above 2% ruled out — [NBER w33777](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777)
- [BG 2026-02] IND (LinkedIn Economic Graph), DESCRIPTIVE. "Changes in real interest rates account for most of the variation in SWE hiring over the past decade." The alignment of national, tech and SWE hiring "suggests the slowdown is driven mainly by macroeconomic forces, not a disproportionate impact from AI on entry-level SWE roles". Entry-level SWE hiring "did not rebound at the end of 2025", which is "concerning … but it is not enough to conclude that AI is the cause" — [LinkedIn, U.S. Software Engineer Talent Landscape, Feb 2026](https://delivery-p143253-e1476319.adobeaemcloud.com/adobe/assets/urn:aaid:aem:93a60f6f-0ea7-4eb2-864f-b0b0261b9afe/original/as/original.pdf). COI: LinkedIn is owned by Microsoft (GitHub Copilot vendor).
- [BG 2026-01] WP (Economic Innovation Group; Iscenko & Curto Millet, "Looking for the Ladder"). Lightcast postings data (reportedly 238 million US postings) show vacancies in the most AI-exposed occupations peaking in spring 2022 and falling before ChatGPT's November 2022 launch, which lines up with Fed hikes from March 2022 (unverified: seen only via secondary coverage) — [governance.fyi](https://www.governance.fyi/p/marc-andreessen-is-right-that-ai); [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/articles/entry-level-jobs-were-actually-140500075.html). COI: the authors are described in coverage as Google economists (Google is an AI vendor). The Canaries authors cite this work and added the interest-rate control in response — [Canaries Aug 2026 PDF](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf)
- [BG 2026-03-20] Crane & Soto on **post-Covid over-hiring**: coder-intensive industry employment "is right in line with where it 'should' be" given post-Covid dynamics, "consistent with a return to trend following Covid overhiring". In their CES table, software publishing (NAICS 5132) growth went from 8.79 (pre-Covid) and 10.42 (Feb 2020–Oct 2022) to 0.05 post-ChatGPT. On **Section 174** (5-year amortisation of domestic R&D from 2022): "it is not clear if this change had any material impact". They list as open questions whether "remote or offshore coding services providers [are] more vulnerable" — [Crane & Soto](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026018pap.pdf)
- [BG 2025] PRESS/commentary on **Section 174**: the change "could have contributed to fewer software engineers hired in the US 2023-2025" but "was likely not the main cause" — [The Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-pulse-section-174-is-reversed). A 2025 US tax bill mostly reverses it for US-based developers — [LeadDev](https://leaddev.com/hiring/end-section-174-means-software-developer-hiring)
- [BG 2026-02-24] GOV (Dallas Fed, Davis), CORRELATIONAL. Since fall 2022, employment in computer systems design and related services fell 5% while total US employment rose about 2.5%, and wages in that industry rose 16.7%. Across 205 occupations, post-2022 wage growth shows no relationship with AI exposure on average — [Dallas Fed](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224)
- [NEW 2026-07-07] International organisation report (OECD Employment Outlook 2026), DESCRIPTIVE/review. Young entrants, especially young graduates, face rising unemployment relative to other workers, but the rise "began well before generative AI spread". The evidence base is mostly US, and the OECD does not conclude that AI is responsible (from search snippets; the OECD pages returned HTTP 403) — [OECD Employment Outlook 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2026/07/oecd-employment-outlook-2026_a41e8b9f.html); [OECD blog, Aug 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/blogs/2026/08/young-people-labour-markets-and-ai-what-oecd-evidence-shows.html)
- [BG 2026] WP (Örebro University WP 2/2026, Sweden; unverified details). Uses the Riksbank's rate hikes and the ChatGPT launch as a timing test and attributes the broad decline in Swedish postings to monetary tightening — [Örebro WP 2-2026](https://www.oru.se/globalassets/oru-sv/institutioner/hh/workingpapers/workingpapers2026/wp-2-2026.pdf)
- [BG 2026-01] WP (arXiv 2601.02554, unverified): "AI-exposed jobs deteriorated before ChatGPT" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02554)
- [NEW 2026-10-11] PRESS: McKinsey Global Institute says the earlier decline in software developers was not solely due to AI, pointing to the 2022 hiring peak and post-pandemic pullback — [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/11/ai-software-developer-jobs.html). COI: McKinsey sells AI consulting.

### Inferences
- The most defensible reading as of Nov 2026: (a) a large part of the 2022–2024 collapse in software and entry-level tech hiring is macro (rates, post-Covid correction, possibly remote work), since postings peaked in early 2022 and hiring tracks real rates. (b) On top of that, several designs isolate a smaller occupation- or age-specific shortfall for coders and young workers that coincides with ChatGPT, is not explained by industry shocks or interest-rate exposure, and in ADP data widened through mid-2026. That component is the AI-relevant risk.
- Section 174 is unlikely to be the main driver. The Canaries gap survives excluding tech firms and computer occupations, Crane & Soto call its impact unclear, and it was largely reversed in 2025, so it is not a forward-looking risk factor for 2028–2029.
- The remote-work explanation competes with AI, and the two are entangled: teleworkable occupations are disproportionately AI-exposed (Canaries fn. 26). Neither can be cleanly separated with current data.
- Magnitude is the weakest link. ADP shows −19% while the ACS shows about −2% with a CI that includes zero, so the true economy-wide effect on young developers could be much smaller than headlines suggest.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed causal study specific to software developers was found.
- No study quantifies offshoring (for example to India or Eastern Europe) as a driver of US or Dutch junior-developer hiring. Section 174 has no formal causal estimate.
- No causal study for the Netherlands; Dutch evidence is correlational (see Q4).
- The OECD Employment Outlook 2026 chapter text and numbers could not be opened (HTTP 403), and the Iscenko & Curto Millet primary was not accessed.

## 3. "Computer programmers" vs "software developers": BLS data, projections, the March 2025 Washington Post analysis and 2026 updates

### Takeaway
BLS treats the two occupations very differently. **Computer programmers** (110,800 jobs in 2025) are projected to fall 7% over 2025–35, with AI automation of "repetitive programming tasks" named as a reason. **Software developers** (1,717,800 jobs in 2025) are projected to grow 10%, down from +15.8% (2024–34) and +17% (2023–33) in earlier projection vintages. The Washington Post's March 2025 finding of a 27.5% CPS-based fall in programmer employment rests on a small, noisy survey cell. Task data suggest the occupational label matters less than the task mix.

Successive BLS vintages are getting less optimistic about developer growth, though they still project growth.
- The BLS split rewards the task bundle labelled "developer" (requirements, design, integration, testing and security around code) over the bundle labelled "programmer" (writing code to specification). Crane & Soto's finding that the labels overlap heavily in practice cautions against reading this as "coding is dead". The more defensible reading is that writing code alone is the part BLS expects to be automated.
- QA analysts and testers (+6%) are projected to grow more slowly than developers (+10%). Testing as a stand-alone job title is not a growth haven in the BLS view, even if testing and verification skills matter inside developer roles.

### Gaps
- The occupation-level BLS AI exposure categories (XLSX) were not accessible, so whether BLS rates programmers or developers "Very high" is unverified.
- The original Washington Post article was not accessed. Its author, exact CPS series and confidence intervals are unverified.
- BLS OEWS May 2025 employment levels by occupation (versus the OOH matrix figures) were not separately extracted.
- There is no Dutch or EU equivalent of the programmer/developer occupational split with projections. ROA/UWV use "software- en applicatieontwikkelaars" as one group (see Q4).

## 4. Netherlands and EU: data, forecasts and policy studies; outlook for HBO-ICT graduates around 2027–2030

### Takeaway
Dutch data show an ICT labour market that is **cooling from "very tight" towards balance, not collapsing**. ICT vacancies have fallen since 2023 (about 18,900 in Q2 2026) while ICT employment keeps rising (589,000 people in ICT occupations). WW (unemployment benefit) claims from ICT occupations rose quickly to nearly 6,400 in July 2026. ROA forecasts "vrijwel geen knelpunten" (virtually no bottlenecks) for software and application developers by 2030, ICT employment growth slowing from 2.5% a year (2019–2024) to 0.3% a year (2025–2030), and "goed" (good) prospects for bachelor techniek en ICT graduates. ROA says AI's effect is not yet captured in its forecasts. Dutch evidence on AI and young workers is correlational and contested. RaboResearch finds −13% youth employment in GenAI-exposed occupations, which economists (Tilburg/Utrecht, with CPB staff) attribute mainly to monetary tightening, and a CBS-microdata study finds that "for most starters AI does not yet change the labour market".

### Inferences
- For an HBO-ICT graduate entering around 2028–2029, the base-case Dutch outlook (ROA/UWV, made before most AI effects could show up) is a market that is still decent but no longer extremely tight. Bachelor techniek en ICT prospects are "goed", but software and application developers are forecast to move to "virtually no bottlenecks", and employment growth slows from 2.5% to 0.3% a year. In practice, employers will be choosier, and juniors compete more with mediors and with each other.
- Downside risk is not in the forecasts. ROA says AI's effect is "hard to capture". RaboResearch's ICT-sector datum (value added +6.5% while youth employment −11%) and UWV's rising ICT WW counts are consistent with an entry-level squeeze, but also with rates and bank reorganisations. The causal question is as open in NL as in the US.
- European developer-posting indices (Germany −14.6% year on year to a new low; France −7.3%) are weaker than the US. Given the Netherlands' exposure to the German economy, the US 2026 rebound is a weak guide to Dutch junior demand.
- Cnossen's CBS microdata result ("barely negative" for hbo-ICT through the 2024 cohort) is the most direct Dutch evidence for the student's own group. It is reassuring for the period to 2024 but cannot speak to 2026–2029.

### Gaps

## 5. Wage premia and hiring signals for AI skills (PwC, Lightcast, LinkedIn, OECD)

### Takeaway
Vendor and consultancy reports show large and rising pay premia for AI skills: PwC 62% in 2026, Lightcast 28% (about $18,000) in postings. AI-titled roles drove 37% of the 2025–26 US software-postings recovery. These are correlational and come from parties with COI. Independent wage evidence is more muted: no average relationship between AI exposure and wage growth across 205 US occupations (Dallas Fed), and adjustment through employment rather than base pay (Canaries). Experience appears to be the variable that matters: AI exposure hurts wage growth where little tacit, experience-based knowledge is needed and is neutral to positive where experience premia are high.

### Inferences
- The premium studies measure being in (or advertising) AI-skill jobs. They do not measure the return to a junior learning to use AI tools instead of learning to code. PwC's own entry-level finding points the other way from "tool skills": exposed entry-level roles increasingly demand senior-style judgement. Taken together with Indeed (senior-heavy recovery), Dallas Fed (experience premium protects wages), Canaries (tacit knowledge protects employment) and Humlum & Vestergaard (new tasks are oversight and integration), the most consistent signal is that employers pay for **judgement over AI output**: reviewing, verifying, integrating and specifying. That judgement is usually built on hands-on experience.
- For the student's practice split this suggests (inference, not tested in any study found): treat hand-coding fundamentals as the base that makes review and verification credible, and deliberately build the "senior-like" skills that entry-level postings now ask for (testing and verification, design, reading and critiquing others' and AI's code, requirements). Avoid choosing one over the other. No labour-market study directly estimates the optimal split.
- All headline premium numbers (PwC 62%, Lightcast 28%) come from parties that sell AI or labour-market services and are cross-sectional. Treat them as direction-of-travel signals, not causal returns.

### Gaps
- No Netherlands- or EU-specific AI-skill wage premium was found (PwC country cuts for NL were not accessible), and no Dutch data exist on pay for juniors with vs without AI skills.
- The OECD Employment Outlook 2025 and 2026 quantitative AI findings (e.g. AI-related hiring shares by country) could not be extracted.
- No study measures returns to specific AI-era skills (code review, specification, verification, testing) for entry-level developers. Evidence is limited to posting language and occupation-level aggregates.
- Lightcast's 2026 update status and LinkedIn's 68% entry-level AI-engineer figure are unverified.
