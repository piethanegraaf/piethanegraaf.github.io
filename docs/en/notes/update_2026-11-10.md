## 1. New since 8 November 2026

### Albadarin (2026), doctoral dissertation, University of Eastern Finland. NEW, [DISSERTATION], via secondary

- **Source:** university Q&A published on Phys.org on 10 Nov 2026; the dissertation itself was not opened. — [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-qa-exploring-generative-ai-impact.html)
- **Title:** *Capturing the impact of generative AI on higher-order thinking, creativity, and cognitive dynamics during debugging* (Yazid Albadarin, Faculty of Science, Forestry and Technology).
- **Three studies:**
    1. a systematic review of 14 empirical studies of ChatGPT in education;
    2. a **quasi-experiment with 152 university students**: one group used generative AI (Llama 3.1) while programming and debugging, the other worked without AI. Data: assessments, surveys, interviews and a **retention test seven weeks later**;
    3. 132 students, cognitive network analysis of five critical-thinking skills.
- **Findings, as described in the Q&A:**
    - the AI group did better and faster on some debugging tasks, but the group without AI **retained more knowledge after seven weeks**; in the researcher's words, "better performance does not necessarily lead to better learning";
    - AI use went with stronger links between comprehension, application and creativity; working without AI with stronger links between analysis and evaluation;
    - AI may support some forms of creativity but does not clearly improve overall creativity.
- **Not reported in the Q&A:** scores, effect sizes, how the groups were formed, and whether the retention test was taken without AI.
- **Grade:** B (quasi-experimental). The seven-week delay is the longest so far for programming in higher education.
- **To do:** read the dissertation for the figures before citing numbers.

### Stanford SCALE (2026), "The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A November 2026 Review". NEW, [EVIDENCE REVIEW], primary read

- **Citation:** Walker, Fesler, Martinez Claeys, Agnew & Loeb. SCALE Initiative, Stanford Graduate School of Education, 10 Nov 2026. https://doi.org/10.26300/80wn-ca81 — [SCALE](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/evidence-base-review/evidence-base-ai-k12-november-review)
- **Method:** 868 papers added to the SCALE repository between 1 Oct 2025 and 30 Jun 2026; 201 were RCTs or quasi-experiments and 97 went to human review. An LLM pre-screened them on What Works Clearinghouse criteria, and at least two researchers reviewed each causal paper. **32 new papers met the bar for strong causal evidence**, 52 in total with the 20 of the March 2026 review. Most of the repository is arXiv preprints without peer review.
- **Despite the title, most new papers are from higher education.**
- **Conclusions:**
    - AI "appears to be better for learning when students use it for learning support than when they use it for producing work, although neither type of use consistently improves learning";
    - outsourcing academic work to AI can reduce performance when students later work without AI;
    - AI tools can improve the learning experience even when they do not improve learning outcomes;
    - the evidence base does not yet show whether AI tools can produce long-term learning gains.
- **Also noted:** a Brazilian study in which students with traditional resources scored higher on a surprise conceptual test 45 days later; and new US college studies in which general-purpose chatbots used for learning support improved performance without AI, which conflicts with the Turkish high-school findings (Bastani).
- **Use in §2.8:** an independent synthesis that agrees with "performance with AI is not learning". It is not a meta-analysis and pools no effect.

### Chen & Stratton (2026), "Artificial Intelligence in the Firm: Bottlenecks in Software Production". NEW VERSION, [WORKING PAPER], primary read

- **Citation:** Fiona Chen & James Stratton, Harvard University. Job-market paper; current version 9 Nov 2026, first version 7 Jan 2026. — [PDF](https://fion.ac/jellyfish.pdf); coverage of the revision: [BERI, 7 Nov 2026](https://www.beri.net/article/harvard-jellyfish-study-ai-coding-agents-code-review-bottleneck-output-employment-engineering-org-design)
- **Data:** proprietary telemetry from the engineering-analytics platform Jellyfish: 445 million work events (GitHub, Jira, Google Calendar, AI-tool usage) of **720,542 workers at 655 firms**, January 2021 – August 2026.
- **Design:** variation in when firms adopted two generations of tools, assistants (Copilot, Cursor) and agents. Quasi-experimental; adoption is not random.
- **Results, 12 months after adoption:**
    - assistants: lines of code +30.4%, commits +16.8%, pull requests +20.9%;
    - agents, in addition: lines of code +44.0%, commits +36.0%, pull requests opened +35.7%;
    - final output: resolved Jira issues −1.5% (assistants) and **+13.7% (agents, borderline significant)**; epics +3.4% and +18.0%; no effect on the predicted size of issues;
    - **review bottleneck:** after agent adoption, the time from review submission to approval rose **56.1%** (backlog wait +59.9%, active review +58.5%);
    - employment: assistants +3.0% (changes below −4.7% ruled out); agents −2.6% (changes below −15.6% ruled out). The authors find no detectable effect on employment.
- **Main claim (abstract):** "Code review becomes the bottleneck."
- **COI:** the data partner (Jellyfish, which sells engineering analytics) reviewed the manuscript only for proprietary information, not for its scientific content. Funding: Stripe Economics of AI Fellowship, Harvard Kennedy School Stone Program, Schmidt Sciences AI at Work.
- **Version warning:** the August version (718 firms, data to March 2026) reported other figures: about +30% lines of code, PR-to-merge time +49%, no significant change in resolved Jira issues (via secondary: [letsdatascience](https://letsdatascience.com/news/harvard-study-finds-ai-coding-agents-raise-code-volume-not-o-ea78729b)). Cite the version of 9 November.
- **Grade:** B (working paper, quasi-experimental). The first independent measurement of the review bottleneck; §2.9 rested on vendor statements before.

## 2. Missed by the searches of 7 November (in window, 1 May – 7 Nov 2026)

### Bergh, Tag, Vassar & Renzella (2026), "Your Programming Students' Cognition with ChatGPT: Higher Performance, Lower Retention, and Reduced Ownership". MISSED, [PREPRINT], abstract only

- **Citation:** arXiv:2609.21194 [cs.CY], v1 18 Sep 2026. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21194)
- **Design:** controlled between-subjects experiment; 59 undergraduate computing students enrolled, 55 analysed; three introductory C tasks with ChatGPT or with conventional web search. **Randomization is not stated in the abstract.**
- **Results:**
    - coding scores **89% vs. 69%** (ChatGPT higher);
    - recall **41% vs. 53%** right away and **39% vs. 52%** after 48 hours (ChatGPT lower); the loss over 48 hours did not differ between the groups;
    - self-reported mental effort rose less across tasks with ChatGPT (Holm-adjusted p = .047);
    - **ownership:** students credited themselves with 45% of their submitted code with ChatGPT vs. 81% without;
    - pupil and heart-rate measures: no significant differences, with substantial data loss.
- **Grade:** B for now (A, small, if assignment was randomized; check the full text).

### Lepp (2026), "Patterns of Learner-AI Interaction and Academic Performance in an Object-Oriented Programming Course". MISSED, [PREPRINT, accepted at FIE 2026], abstract only

- **Citation:** Marina Lepp. arXiv:2607.24755; accepted for IEEE Frontiers in Education (FIE) 2026. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24755)
- **Design:** survey of **210 first-year students in an OOP course**. Generative AI was allowed for coursework and **prohibited in assessments**. Cluster analysis of usage.
- **Results:** students used AI more for explanations and debugging than for code generation. Five interaction profiles, including a "smart" high-usage profile (little code generation, much conceptual and debugging support). The profiles differed in perceived difficulty, self-assessed understanding, trust in AI code and norms, but **not significantly in assessment performance**.
- **Author's conclusion:** self-directed AI use alone does not produce measurable learning gains; pedagogically guided, process-aware support is needed.
- **Grade:** C (survey, observational). Same author as Lepp & Kaimre (2026), already cited.

### O'Neill (2026), audit of AI-in-education meta-analyses. MISSED, [PREPRINT], via secondary

- **Source:** EdArXiv/OSF preprint (osf.io/preprints/edarxiv/n29zd_v3), not opened; posting date not verified. — [Hechinger Report, 26 Oct 2026](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-ai-education-meta-analysis/); [Education Week, 2 Nov 2026](https://www.edweek.org/technology/the-research-on-ai-in-education-is-a-mess/2026/11)
- **Author:** Patrick O'Neill, Ivy Tech Community College.
- **Scope:** 14 of the 39 AI-in-education meta-analyses that reported positive effects; together more than 3,000 citations.
- **Problems reported:** "impossible statistics", "hallucinated references" and "overstated conclusions"; underlying studies that cite generative-AI papers from before the technology was public; comparisons of AI-supported study with no study at all; no correction for publication bias. **About two-thirds of the calls for papers seemed to discourage null or negative results.** One of the meta-analyses was retracted.
- **Recalculation:** his own preliminary meta-analysis of **44 well-designed experiments** averaged out to roughly zero, with results ranging from very helpful to very harmful. No effect size is given in the coverage.
- **Use in §2.8 and R18:** independent support for Boolzen et al. (2026): uncorrected pooled effects are not evidence that AI improves learning.

### Demirer, Musolff & Yang (2026), "Writing Code vs. Shipping Code: Productivity Effects Across Generations of AI Coding Tools". MISSED, [WORKING PAPER], primary read (NBER page)

- **Citation:** NBER Working Paper 35275, May 2026, revised September 2026. https://doi.org/10.3386/w35275 — [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35275)
- **Data and design:** more than 500,000 GitHub developers with AI-usage telemetry, plus four software marketplaces; matched event study.
- **Results:** cumulative effects on commits of +30% (autocomplete), +180% (interactive agents) and **+240% (autonomous agents)**. For autonomous agents, the effect falls to +80% for the number of projects and +30% for releases. Estimated elasticity of substitution between AI and human effort: 0.23. A sharp rise in new apps in the marketplaces, without a rise in total usage. The authors conclude that task-level gains have so far translated only partly into shipped and used software.
- **Grade:** B (working paper, quasi-experimental).
